## ðŸ“ˆ Chapter 1: Mastering Growth

This chapter provides an executive summary of the three growth barriers and the outcomes from implementing the Rockefeller Habits.

### ðŸ’¼ Executive Summary: Three Barriers to Growth

Growing firms face three common barriers:

1. **Executive Team Growth:** Leaders' abilities to delegate and predict.
2. **Systems and Structures:** Handling complexity with growth.
3. **Market Dynamics:** Navigating increasingly tricky larger markets.

This chapter outlines the positive outcomes of overcoming these barriers through the Rockefeller Habits.

### ðŸš€ The "Gazelle" Phenomenon

- Only **4% of US firms** survive the transition from small business to growing firm, termed "gazelles."
- **Gazelles** grow at least **20% a year for four years in a row**.
- They fuel over two-thirds of the world's economic growth and almost all job creation.

### ðŸŽ¯ Delegating to Others

Delegation is a critical leadership skill. Most firms remain small (96% have fewer than ten employees) because entrepreneurs struggle to delegate. The key insight is: **if you can't afford people to run the business for you, you have a job, not a business.** Effective delegation involves:

- Choosing the **right person or team** (one great person replaces three good ones).
- A **four-step process**:
  1. Pinpoint tasks.
  2. Create a measurement system.
  3. Provide feedback.
  4. Give timed recognition and reward.

### ðŸ—ï¸ Systems and Structures

Complexity grows exponentially with organizational expansion. Systems and structures are logical responses to this complexity. As a company scales:

- From 2 to 10 employees: Requires better phone systems and structured space.
- To 50 employees ($10M in revenue): Requires an accounting system to track profitability by projects, customers, or products.
- Above 50 employees (or $50M in revenue): Information-technology systems need upgrading, and systems should tie into a single customer/employee database.

Three types of organizational charts are crucial:

| Chart Type | Purpose | Key Characteristics |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Accountability Chart** | Ensures clear ownership for all projects, line items, priorities, and processes. | No "to be determined" positions; specific names in positions; "off org chart" roles for senior individual contributors. |
| **Work Process Charts** | Maps critical workflows (e.g., customer acquisition, project flow, employee selection, billing/collections). | Keep accountability chart clean; establish 4-9 workflow charts; clean up one dysfunctional process per quarter. |
| **Almost Matrix** | Shows relationships between organizational functions and business units (product lines, customer niches, locations, subsidiaries). | Addresses tensions between functional leaders and business unit heads; typically, sales report to business unit leaders; functional leaders act as coaches. |

**Scaling Up (Rockefeller Habits 2.0)** introduces one-page tools: the **Function Accountability Chart (FACe)** and **Process Accountability Chart (PACe)**.

### ðŸ“Š Data Drives Prediction

The goal of systems and structures is **predictability**. Companies need to know their current position and project future performance (weekly, monthly, quarterly, annually). This allows for timely intervention when targets are missed.

- **McKinney Lumber Example:** Joe McKinney scaled his family business to $90 million by focusing on metrics. He popularized a "Critical Number" for plant productivity, tracking daily profit indicators. This provided a "firm foundation to know where you areâ€”even if you donâ€™t like the answer."
- **Mostly Muffins Example:** Molly Wilmot tracked progress daily, identifying issues faster than monthly accounting statements. This allowed for immediate team action.

As companies scale, accuracy in data becomes critical. A 2% error rate on 1,000 units is a miss of 20, requiring tighter system tolerances.

### ðŸŽ¯ Top 5 Priorities

Maintaining focus is crucial, especially beyond 30 employees. Many scaleups use a **Top 5 and Top-1-of-5 priority list** for each quarter, aligning individual priorities with company goals.

- **Carney Example:** John Carney's company (learning solutions) scaled significantly. He used laminated 8.5x11 sheets displaying corporate Top 5 priorities, 8 core values, and individual Top 5 priorities for the quarter, serving as a simple performance appraisal system.
- **Visibility:** These visible priorities signal focus and good business practices to clients, bankers, and prospective employees.

### ðŸ¤ Meeting Rhythm

Quarterly themes create focus and motivation, but a **daily and weekly rhythm of meetings** ensures accountability and alignment. Rockefeller himself had daily luncheon meetings with his executives for 19 years while building Standard Oil.

- **Daily Huddle (15 minutes max per group):**
  - Celebrates progress.
  - Identifies roadblocks.
  - Keeps people informed, prevents issues from cluttering weekly agendas.
- **"Situation Room":** A dedicated huddle area with walls mapped with top priorities, core values, metric charts, and market data keeps executive teams focused.

Alan Rudy of Express-Med noted that "Routine sets you free," transforming his $65M firm by instituting a daily huddle rhythm. Effective meetings are short, punchy, structured, and avoid problem-solving, which should be taken offline.

### ðŸŒ Market Dynamics

Understanding and responding to market trends is vital. Leaders must think differently than competitors and embrace market forces.

- **Steve Smith (The Lawrence Group):** Shifted mindset from "designing a building" to "designing a business" specializing in radio stations, healthcare, and university arenas. Embraced the Internet to create a national network of architectural practices.
- **Growth and Focus:**
  - Under $10 million: Tendency to focus externally, but internal habits are crucial.
  - Over $10 million: Organizational complexity draws attention inward, but external market focus becomes even more important. External assistance can help maintain external focus.

#### ðŸ’² Financial Focus Across Growth Stages

| Revenue Stage | Key Driver / Focus | Cash Flow Status | Challenges / Considerations |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Start-up to $1-2M | **Revenue** (getting market interested) | Self-funding (friends, family, fools) | High risk, low predictability |
| $1M to $10M | **Revenue + Cash** | Risk of "growing broke" (cash rapidly consumed) | Experimentation to find market focus/position can be costly; internal complexities start. |

The journey of a growing business is to create a **predictable engine in an unpredictable world**. This involves continuous leadership growth, installing systems, and capitalizing on market dynamics for profitable scaling.

### ðŸŒ± Grow Thyself

Tough times offer CEOs opportunities for re-evaluation. Molly Wilmot (Mostly Muffins) redefined her role to focus on connecting with people and selling the company's vision, delegating numerical management to structures and measurements.

Alan Rudy (Express-Med) backed off day-to-day oversight, trained himself on personal style, and hired a president, freeing him to focus on new projects and acquisitions.

## ðŸ¤ Chapter 2: Mastering the Right People Doing the Right Things Right

This chapter provides an overall framework for three key decisions that drive business value, aligning with Jim Collins' research.

### ðŸ’¡ Three Basic Executive Decisions

1. Do we have the **Right People**?
2. Are we doing the **Right Things**?
3. Are we doing those **Things Right**?

### ðŸ§‘â€ðŸ¤â€ðŸ§‘ The Right People

The Container Store, a #1 "Best Companies to Work For," exemplifies having the Right People. Their philosophy:

- One great person can replace three good people.
- Pay 50-100% more than typical retailers (fewer people needed).
- Provide over 200 hours of first-year training (vs. 10 hours industry standard).

This approach leads to increased loyalty and scales the business effectively. Key questions to identify Right People:

- Would you enthusiastically rehire each person on your team?
- Do executive team members and key employees have the potential to be the best in their position in 3-5 years? (Note: Right People might be in the wrong positions).

#### ðŸŽ¯ Hiringâ€”Selling the Vision

Hiring is a numbers game. Best firms attract a large pool of high-quality applicants by having a stellar reputation. To improve hiring:

- **Source a large pool:** Aim for 50 high-quality applicants for executive positions. Leverage "A" networks.
- **Top 10 list:** Email 10-20 A-players in your network with a two-paragraph summary of your firm, position, and ideal candidate. Follow up.
- **Sell your vision:** Market your firm to potential employees with the same vigor as to customers. Example: A client increased applicants threefold by changing ads from a generic description to one selling the vision and culture ("WHEN WAS THE LAST TIME YOU HAD FUN AT WORK?").
- **Referral Bonuses:** Offer significant bonuses ($5000 vs. $500) for A-player employee referrals, paid in stages (10% hire, 40% 6-month, 50% 1-year).

#### ðŸ”Ž The Selection Process

Interviewing is perilous due to a slight negative correlation between interview performance and job fit. Structured interviews are key. Recommended books: "Topgrading" and "Who: The A Method for Hiring" by Brad and Geoff Smart.

- **Testing:** More accurate and objective than interviews. Use professional testing for leader and executive hires (e.g., Bartell & Bartell, Bigby Havis & Associates). Test for culture fit, positive outlook, and emotional maturity.
- **Assessment-center approach:** Present candidates with 3-4 business challenges and give them 30-60 minutes to work through solutions. Discuss their approach to gauge thinking and collaboration style. This can even yield business insights.
- **Work experience:** Best way to select is to have candidates work with you for several weeks (temp-to-perm, consulting basis, board membership). Promoting from within or hiring former colleagues is highly effective.

The CEO's most important job is to get the **right people in the right positions** and to remove the wrong people quickly. A strong executive team and coach are vital for identifying problem people that the CEO might overlook.

### âš™ï¸ Right Things Model

The "Right Things Right" model (Figure 2-1) is a framework integrating fundamental business decisions, relationships, and functions. It helps explain business fundamentals to employees and choose quarterly priorities.

![Figure 2-1: Right Things Right Model with Lead People, Manage Activities, and Accountability Pyramid](https://i.imgur.com/vHq1KzO.png)#### Model Components:

- **Right Things (Left Side - People/Relationships):** Focus on **Customers, Employees, Shareholders**. Goal: **Get, Keep, Grow** relationships.
- **Things Right (Right Side - Activities/Transactions):** Focus on **Make/Buy, Sell, Keep Good Records**. Goal: Do activities **Better, Faster, Cheaper**.

**Underlying Vision:** The entire model rests on the company's vision, represented by the Planning Pyramid (bottom of Figure 2-1), which includes Core Values, Purpose, Targets, Goals, Actions, Schedule, and Accountability.

#### Key Questions for "Right Things" (Economic Model):

- Do you have a **viable economic model** (can you make real money)?
- Do you have a product/service enough customers value?
- Have you determined your **X factor** (controllable differentiator that matters to customers and provides market advantage)?
- Can you be the **best in your chosen sandbox**?

#### Key Questions for "Things Right" (Management Practices):

- Do you have management practices and processes to leverage market opportunities?
- Are habits and disciplines in place to maintain competitive advantage?
- Is the organization structured to maximize employee productivity?
- Can you deliver a consistent service/product offering?

#### Success Indicators:

- You're doing the "Right Things" if **revenue or market share are growing at twice the market rate**.
- "Right Things" requires bold leadership for strategy and direction pivots (e.g., Facebook's desktop to mobile).
- "Things Right" requires capable management to maintain disciplines. Technology can augment management, allowing people to focus on leading.

#### People vs. Activities (Leadership vs. Management):

A business is "people doing activities." Leaders lead people; they don't manage them. It's crucial to separate the person from their activities and hold people accountable for results.

#### Stakeholders (Left Side - Balance Sheet):

- **Customers:** Need to fill basic needs, differentiate from competition.
- **Employees (including suppliers/sub-contractors):** Need competencies to meet customer needs.
- **Shareholders:** Value created through meeting needs.

The goal on the "Right Things" side is to **get, keep, and grow all three relationships**, building a great reputation. A great reputation makes it easier to achieve these outcomes.

#### Activities (Right Side - Income Statement/P&L):

- **Make/Buy** (COO function).
- **Sell** (VP Sales & Marketing function).
- **Keep Good Records** (CFO function).

The goal on the "Things Right" side is to do these activities **better, faster, and cheaper**, lowering costs relative to sales price and improving the value proposition to increase profitability ("buy low, sell high, and keep good records").

#### Balancing Act:

There's a constant balance between the left and right sides of the model: driving revenue vs. profitability, having enough people vs. enough activities, protecting reputation vs. increasing productivity. This balance is critical for managing priorities and rests on the company's "pinpoint vision."

### ðŸ› ï¸ Putting the Model to Work (Rockefeller Habits)

#### ðŸŽ¯ Rockefeller Habit #1â€”Priorities

To determine the #1 priority for a quarter, consider the six circles of the "Right Things Right" model as potential focus areas. Choose one area on the "People" side and one on the "Process" side that needs the most attention.

- **Left Side Driver:** Specify "get, keep, or grow" (e.g., "increase by 25% (grow) business with top four customers").
- **Right Side Driver:** Specify "better, faster, or cheaper" (e.g., "reduce time by 50% (faster) to properly bill clients").

Focusing energy on one area at a time creates momentum across all interconnected areas. Visual analogies: spinning plates or juggling balls help illustrate the need for sequential focus. Accountability for each circle (customers, shareholders, sales engine) must be clear.

#### ðŸ“ˆ Rockefeller Habit #2â€”Data

Daily and weekly metrics for all six business areas are needed to monitor progress and predict future outcomes. Mid-market firms often lack accurate customer feedback (left side) and sales funnel data (right side, due to sales resistance to measurement beyond top line).

#### ðŸ¥ Rockefeller Habit #3â€”Rhythm

The six circles guide weekly meeting structures. Operations, sales, and accounting need their own daily/weekly rhythms. The executive team needs rhythms for meeting with customers and employees. Public companies have rhythms around shareholders.

### ðŸ›ï¸ Organizational Structure Guided by the Six Circles

The six circles help adapt organizational structure for growth:

- Around $10M revenue: "Sell" splits into sales and marketing (marketing's key measurable: lead generation). "Make or Buy" splits into operations and R&D. "Keep Good Records" splits into accounting and finance.
- "People" side: More specific functional areas for employees (HR), customer-focused teams, and shareholder relations (for public firms).

The "Right People Doing the Right Things Right" model provides a comprehensive framework for leaders to drive business value, with specific tools detailed in subsequent chapters.

### ðŸ“œ One-Page Strategic Plan (OPSP) - Detailed Explanation

The OPSP is the Planning Pyramid turned on its side, organizing strategic vision and ensuring all pieces are connected. Its physical structure enforces prioritization and conciseness. It integrates frameworks from Jim Collins, Gary Hamel, Jack Stack, and Stephen Covey.

The OPSP incorporates Jim Collins' "preserve the core/stimulate progress" duality:

- **First three columns (Core):** Describe unchanging elements over time.
- **Balance of the plan (Dynamic):** Focus on stimulating progress to meet market trends, opportunities, and challenges.

The OPSP is an internal tool for getting technical aspects of the strategic plan correct, rather than crafting marketing messages.

Christensen's "innovator's dilemma" highlights how market leaders can be blinded by current reality.

#### âœ… SWT Instead (for Senior Leaders)

SWT replaces SWOT for senior leaders to identify internal Strengths and Weaknesses while exploring broader external Trends beyond their industry or geography. Strategic planning involves two distinct activities:

- **Strategic Thinking:** Big-picture ideas.
- **Execution Planning:** Making ideas happen.

The SWT is for strategic thinking, helping leaders face brutal facts about internal capabilities and external threats. The analogy "It's Time to Build" (from Andreessen Horowitz) frames this: a rock-solid foundation (Strengths, Weaknesses) and getting the roof on quickly (understanding Trends).

#### ðŸ“ˆ Trends

Senior leaders must look beyond immediate opportunities and threats to major global trends in technology, distribution, product innovation, markets, and consumer/social developments. This includes anticipating disruptive technologies (e.g., robotics) and global competitors.

- Choose **4-6 trends** most likely to impact your industry and business, listed on the bottom of the OPSP. These "stimulate progress" for the right side of the OPSP.
- To enhance trend awareness, senior leaders should attend annual tradeshows in a completely different industry (e.g., industrial attending consumer electronics show).
- Recommended reading: Peter Diamandis's "Abundance: The Future is Better Than You Think" and Frost & Sullivan's "Mega Trends" insights.

#### ðŸ’ª Core Strengths and Weaknesses

These are inherent attributes of an organization.

- **Core Strengths (Core Competencies):** Resources and capabilities anchoring strategic advantage (e.g., BIC's competencies allowing pivot from pens to lighters/razors). Leverage strengths, don't try to change who you are.
- **Core Weaknesses:** Inherently weak and not easily fixable attributes, often cultural or foundational (e.g., 3M's "science" culture leading to a direct sales weakness, offset by partnering strength). Avoid weaknesses.

Core strengths and weaknesses are identified for long-term planning and anchor the "preserve the core" side of the OPSP. For BFIS (Ben Franklin International School), Barcelona's attractiveness for expats was a strength, while its physical location was a weakness for expansion.

#### ðŸ§  Mining All Levels for Ideas

Effective strategic planning requires gathering ideas from all organizational levels:

- **Frontline employees & customers:** Use "Start/Stop/Keep" questions.
- **Middle management:** Standard SWOT analysis, top three priorities for quarter/year.
- **Senior team:** SWT analysis (deeper, broader focus on global trends).

Jeff Bezos at Amazon regularly asks his team about new competitors entering their market within the last seven days. The OPSP, SWT, and Vision Summary are vital tools for daily alignment, with "1 percent vision and 99 percent alignment" being the mantra. The goal is to get people aligned to do what needs to be done, not to wordsmith endlessly.

## ðŸŒŸ Chapter 4: Mastering the Use of Core Values

This chapter emphasizes that a strong culture, built on a handful of core values, leads to superior performance, higher employee retention, and a better-aligned organization. It simplifies leadership, reduces the need for extensive policies, and provides a foundation for decision-making.

### ðŸ“œ Rules for a Great Company Culture (from Parenting)

The three keys for a strong cultural foundation are:

1. Have a few rules (core values).
2. Repeat yourself a lot (inculcate values).
3. Act consistently with those rules (live the values).

Hatim Tyabji, in building Verifone from $30M to $600M, used eight core values as his primary leadership tool, repeating them constantly to build a strong global culture.

### ðŸ”Ž Finding the Right Words: Mission to Mars Exercise

Discovering core values can be challenging. Jim Collins' "Mission to Mars" exercise provides a fast and effective way to draft core values (30 minutes to a couple hours for a finished document).

#### Mission to Mars Process:

1. **Gather a representative group:** Employees or senior leadership team.
2. **Identify "emissaries":** Each person lists 5 employees (not in the room) who best embody the good things about the company, through their actions. (For startups, initial founders/employees).
3. **Vote getters:** Determine the top 3 vote-getting employees. (Keep lists confidential.)
4. **Discuss characteristics:** For each top employee, discuss:
   - Who are they?
   - How do they work?
   - What would customers/co-workers say about them?
   - Why are they important/valuable to the organization?
5. **Identify themes:** Jot down what's said. Patterns will emerge, revealing real core values (not just aspirational ones). Collins emphasizes there's no "universally right" set of core values; the key is having them at all.
6. **Refine into phrases:** Word-smith concepts into key phrases and rules.

**Example: Scaling Up Core Values:**

1. **Create Ecstatic Clients**
2. **Honor Intellectual Creatives**
3. **Everyone an Entrepreneur**
4. **Practice What We Preach**
5. **Relentless Transparency**

This exercise fosters mutual understanding between leadership and employees, leading to alignment and company thriving. Atlassian's core values, discovered through this method, have remained consistent despite scaling from 50 to 8000+ employees.

### ðŸ“¢ Techniques for Bringing Your Core Values Alive

Defining values is 1% of the effort; the other 99% is keeping them alive. This requires constant repetition and consistent living of the values, going beyond merely posting them.

#### Methods for Inculcating Core Values:

1. **Storytelling:**
   - Use parables to teach and cement bonds between CEO and employees.
   - Explain unusual or cryptic core values with stories.
   - Incorporate storytelling into monthly/quarterly all-employee meetings.
   - Nominate and share examples in weekly executive team meetings.
2. **Recruitment and Selection:**
   - Use core values language in job ads and descriptions to attract aligned candidates.
   - Design interview questions and assessments to test alignment.
   - Interviewers rate candidates on perceived alignment with each core value.
3. **Orientation:**
   - Make core values the major themes of your orientation process.
   - CEO or top executive shares company legends behind each core value during orientation.
   - Example: Sapient's week-long Boot Camp used core values to organize experiential learning, significantly improving retention.
4. **Performance Appraisal:**
   - Use core values as the outline/skeleton for your performance-appraisal system.
   - Link any performance measure to a core value creatively.
   - Organize employee handbooks around core values (like "Ten Commandments").
5. **Recognition and Reward:**
   - Use core values as categories for public recognition and rewards at quarterly/annual meetings or bulletin boards.
   - This generates new stories highlighting core values in action.
6. **Internal Newsletter:**
   - Use a core value as a theme for each issue.
   - Highlight a core value with stories of employees exemplifying it.
7. **Themes:**
   - Core values are a natural source for quarterly or annual themes for corporate improvement efforts (e.g., Milliken Textile using one of six values as a quarterly theme).
   - Conduct audits for alignment with a particular core value, fostering dialogue.
8. **Everyday Management:**
   - Managers and CEOs should constantly relate decisions, reprimands, praise, customer issues, and employee concerns back to core values.
   - These small, daily actions significantly strengthen the company culture.

The principle is similar to teaching a child: **have a few rules, repeat yourself, and be consistent**. This provides clarity, guides conduct, and ensures rules aren't a moving target. While tempting to rush, taking the time to define and live core values is crucial for companies experiencing rapid growth.

## ðŸŽ¯ Chapter 5: Mastering Organizational Alignment And Focus

This chapter emphasizes the need for leadership to clearly articulate the five most important priorities (Top 5) and the single most critical priority (Top 1 of 5) to move the company forward. It stresses that everyone in the organization must align their individual priorities with these company goals to achieve top performance.

### ðŸš« The Problem of Too Many Priorities

An organization with too many priorities effectively has no priorities. Humans tend to avoid difficult tasks. Management must clearly articulate the Top 5 priorities for the company's next level of growth, and each employee must define their own Top 5, aligned with the company's. This alignment is crucial for performance.

#### ðŸ’¡ The Ivy Lee Method: Power of Focus

Consultant Ivy Lee demonstrated the power of focus to Charles Schwab (Bethlehem Steel) decades ago:

1. Write down the **six most important tasks** for the next business day.
2. **Prioritize** them in order of true importance.
3. At the start of the day, work only on **number one** until it's complete.
4. Then move to **number two**, and so on.
5. Don't worry if only one or two tasks are completed; you've worked on the most important ones.

Schwab found this the most profitable lesson, paying Lee $25,000. The key is not just making a list, but identifying and working on the #1 priority first, and reviewing/reprioritizing daily.

### ðŸ“… Establishing a Planning Context for Your Top 5 and Top 1 of 5

Using the Planning Pyramid (Chapter 3) helps align long-term goals with quarterly challenges. To determine short-term Top 5 and Top 1 of 5 priorities:

- Ask: "What do I need to be doing today to keep this company moving towards its plans at the speed the market demands?"
- Adjust time frame based on growth rate:
  - Less than 15% annual growth: Quarter = Year.
  - 20-50% annual growth: Quarter acts like a year.
  - Over 100% annual growth: Month acts like a year.
- Executives must determine their own Top 5 and Top 1 of 5, cascading this process down the organization.
- Regular performance appraisals should be based on these lists.
- Monthly/quarterly all-employee meetings to review Top 5/Top 1 of 5, along with core values, provide "handful of rules" for decisions.

To make Top 5/Top 1 of 5 actionable, use a **Management Accountability Plan (MAP) worksheet** (Figure 5-1). This requires assigning accountability within 24-48 hours for:

- Point person for each priority.
- Deliverables.
- Sub-accountabilities.
- Resource needs.
- Deadlines and sub-deadlines.

This process must be cascaded throughout the organization, with everyone producing a one-page MAP for their major priorities.

Identifying the Top 5 and Top 1 of 5 can be difficult and painful. If it doesn't cause discomfort, you likely haven't pinpointed the true priority. Golfer Tiger Woods' agonizing year of re-learning his swing exemplifies pushing through a difficult #1 priority to achieve long-term greatness.

## ðŸ—£ï¸ Chapter 7: Mastering Customer and Employee Feedback

This chapter outlines the importance of systematic customer and employee feedback to identify and resolve recurring problems ("hassles") within an organization, which often cost employees 40% of their time and lead to lost customers and revenue. It provides a six-point process for resolving identified issues and introduces Smart Numbers and Critical Numbers for objective future-oriented measurement.

### ðŸ˜© The Cost of Recurring Problems

Recurring problems and hassles are the primary reasons people dislike their jobs and customers leave. While challenges can be exhilarating, persistent, unresolved issues wear down an organization, leading to frustration and reduced productivity.

- Recurring problems consume over **40% of the average employee's time**.
- They halt progress as multiple people discuss and attempt to fix issues.
- Systematically gathering data on customer and employee hassles is crucial for improving internal operations, shortening cycle times, and enhancing customer service.

#### ðŸ’¡ Dell Computer's "Hour of Horror"

Michael Dell's early practice at PCs Limited (later Dell Computer) involved employees keeping weekly lists of problems, complaints, concerns, issues, ideas, or suggestions. Dell reviewed these lists for patterns, leading to a "hour of horror" brainstorming session to solve some (but not all) problems. This selective approach, inspired by the concept of compound interest (1% improvement weekly yields significant long-term gains), avoided overwhelming the system.

### ðŸ“ Gathering the Data: Be Encouraging, Be Responsive

To start, conduct a three-part survey for customers and employees:

1. What should \[company name\] **start** doing?
2. What should \[company name\] **stop** doing?
3. What should \[company name\] **keep** doing?

For employees, also consider the customer's perspective. Compile results, brainstorm solutions, and report progress in internal newsletters to pave the way for an ongoing feedback system. Encourage employees to log any recurring problems that cost them more than a minute to deal with, or customer requests that can't be met.

- **Incentivize participation:** Offer weekly prize drawings or raffle tickets for each submitted hassle.
- **Executive example:** Leaders should share their own top 10 hassles.
- **Raw data:** Log all data, even resolved issues, to observe patterns. Avoid summarizing to ensure all contributions are considered (unless personal attacks, which are handled privately).
- **Ritz Carlton Example:** Personnel log all customer/employee problems daily, providing data for quality improvement.

### ðŸš§ Handling the Feedback

The feedback system must be **responsive** to avoid drying up. Address quick-hit solutions immediately. For larger, thornier issues (often IT or billing-related), assign a team and schedule regular updates.

- **Managing volume:** If swamped with input, do not omit, combine, or summarize. Post raw data (e.g., on a website, internal online group).
- **Categorization:** Allow employees to categorize issues by topic boxes.

### ðŸ“Š Reporting Progress

People need to see change happening. Closing the loop on feedback is crucial.

- **Columbia University Business School Example:** Dean posted complaints/suggestions, scrawling "Noted, done" when issues were addressed.
- **Transparency:** Explain why certain issues cannot be addressed (e.g., Sundance natural food store explaining distribution company challenges).
- **Issues-aging report:** Track how long issues have been outstanding. McKinney Lumber used a large roll of paper on a factory wall for visible tracking until resolution.

### ðŸ¤ De-Hassling Etiquette 101 (Six-Point Problem-Solving Guidelines)

These guidelines ensure the de-hassling system is fair, humane, and productive, not a "corporate witch hunt."

1. **Relevancy:** Focus on issues that truly matter, are top importance, affect customers/employees, and represent recurring patterns. Tackle those costing the most time/money.
2. **Be Specific:** Avoid generalities ("communications problems," "system's always down"). Dig for "who, what, when, where, how, why" of hassles. Push for specifics in staff meetings.
3. **Address the Root:** Focus on the cause, not just symptoms. Use the "5 Whys" technique to get to the root cause (e.g., mis-shipped items due to worn-out printer, leading to question of why 5 copies needed).
4. **Focus on the What, Not the Who:** Prevent blame games; 95% of problems are process-related. If a "who" consistently emerges, it may indicate a hiring/training process issue, requiring tough personnel decisions, but still rooted in "what went wrong."
5. **Involve All Those Affected:** Get everyone affected in the same room to discuss the problem, ensuring a true picture and minimizing suboptimization (solving one problem creates others).
6. **Never Backstab:** Do not speak negatively about someone not present (exception: seeking advice before confronting, then bring individual into conversation ASAP). This builds trust and openness.

Implementing these guidelines boosts morale, improves productivity, and prevents productivity drain.

### ðŸ§‘â€ðŸŽ“ Leadership-Development Opportunities

The de-hassling system should not solely burden the executive team. Forming a **mid-level leadership team** for initial screening and problem-solving provides a leadership development opportunity, investing in succession planning, and empowering supervisors to creatively address issues.

### ðŸ“ˆ Daily and Weekly Measures

These measures drive performance, guide priorities, and anticipate problems/opportunities. They help employees answer, "Did I have a good week?"

#### ðŸŽ¯ Critical Numbers

- The company needs **one Critical Number** to align around for the next quarter or year.
- It's the **single most important measurable thing** to accomplish in the next 3-12 months.
- This focus should change regularly, like a weightlifter targeting different muscle groups, to improve various aspects of the organization and keep things interesting.
- **Example:** Dell Computer focused on increasing server sales to PC sales ratio in 2001 to transition to a faster-growth market.
- When choosing a Critical Number from one side of the business (e.g., People/Balance Sheet: reduce employee turnover), pick a **counterbalancing number** from the other side (e.g., Process/P&L: improve gross margins) to prevent unintended negative consequences.

#### ðŸ“Š Smart Numbers

- Executive teams identify **three Smart Numbers** to gain insight into future performance.
- A Smart Number is a **complex ratio** of key indicators (e.g., current sales vs. last year's sales vs. market growth rate) that predicts market share gains or sales effectiveness.
- These weekly measures make the business "smarter" and must be tracked consistently over time for accurate comparisons.

#### ðŸ‘€ Measures for Everyone & Highly Visible

- Every person/team should have **1-2 daily or weekly measures** aligned with company Critical and Smart Numbers. This creates "line of sight"â€”connecting individual efforts to company goals.
- **Example:** Improving customer service rating as a Critical Number led every employee/team to find ways to contribute (e.g., faster response, accurate order-taking).
- **Visibility:** Measurements must be highly visible (large charts, TV screens, whiteboards in offices) and graphical. Employees physically plotting their numbers makes results personal and alive. Displaying last year's results and target lines is helpful.

### ðŸ”® Prediction (Forward Forecasting)

Once daily/weekly measuring is established, move beyond documenting the past to **forward forecasting**. Make educated guesses about future weeks/months. Comparing actual vs. predicted results improves prediction ability and understanding of what drives outcomes.

### ðŸš¨ Situation Room

Create a dedicated "situation room" for the executive team to display:

- Core values and purpose.
- Quarterly and annual priorities.
- Map of geographical territory.
- Large, graphical Smart Numbers and Critical Number displays (someone accountable for updates).

If a dedicated room isn't feasible, use movable easels with whiteboards/corkboards. Proudly displaying this information, even to customers, builds confidence in the firm's management.

## Meeting Recap: Problem-Solving Guidelines

These guidelines are similar to those in conflict resolution and decision-making courses, ensuring effective and humane problem-solving:

- **Relevancy:** Prioritize recurring hassles that impact customers and employees most.
- **Specificity:** Avoid generalizations; seek concrete details (who, what, when, where, how, why).
- **Address the Root:** Use the "5 Whys" technique to find underlying causes, not just symptoms.
- **Focus on What, Not Who:** Most issues are process problems. If a person consistently causes issues, re-evaluate hiring/training.
- **Involve All Affected:** Get all relevant parties in one room to solve problems and prevent suboptimization.
- **Never Backstab:** Maintain trust by not speaking negatively about absent individuals. Address issues directly and transparently.

## ðŸ—“ï¸ Chapter 8: Mastering the Daily and Weekly Meeting

This chapter emphasizes the necessity of a rigorous meeting rhythm (daily, weekly, monthly, quarterly, annual) with specific agendas to enhance team performance, accelerate problem-solving, improve alignment, and ensure effective communication.

### ðŸ•°ï¸ The Importance of Meeting Rhythm

John D. Rockefeller's daily luncheons with key people for 19 years at Standard Oil exemplified the power of frequent interaction to strengthen relationships and conquer business challenges. Similarly, Steve Jobs used daily meetings to turn around Apple. The consistent rhythm of meetings, like jazz improvisation built on a solid beat, keeps everyone aligned and productive.

### â¬†ï¸ More Meetings, Not Less

Growing companies need more, not fewer, meetings. Daily, weekly, monthly, quarterly, and annual meetings build upon each other, driving deliverables for strategic goals. The faster a company grows, the faster its meeting rhythm should pulse:

- &lt;15% growth: Treat a year as a year.
- 20-100% growth: Treat each quarter as a year (new strategy each quarter).
- 100%+ growth: Treat each month as a year (monthly formal strategic meetings, like Netscape).

### â±ï¸ Daily Meetingâ€”An Imperative (5-15 Minutes)

A daily 5-15 minute huddle is non-negotiable for every growing company, even if individuals are in different locations (using conference calls/Zoom). This short routine is critical because it leverages:

- **Peer pressure:** Increases deliverables as individuals face the team daily.
- **Collective intelligence:** Focuses the team's brainpower on issues.
- **Clear communication:** Ensures quick and accurate information flow.

Daily huddles save time by putting out fires early, preventing them from consuming weekly meeting agendas. They provide a predictable time for substantive consultations, eliminating endless "water-cooler" conversations.

#### âš™ï¸ Daily Meeting Structure

- **Focus:** Top 1 Bottleneck.
- **Timing:** Set an irregular time (e.g., 8:08 a.m. or 4:46 p.m.) to ensure punctuality. Use a daily alarm.
- **Setting:** Stand up or perch on stools to keep it short. Leader's office or conference call.
- **Attendees:** "The more the merrier" (Ritz Carlton had 80 headquarters personnel, daily shift meetings at hotels). Express-Med's Alan Rudy had project leaders huddle together. The Scooter Store used a rapid series of tiered daily huddles (frontline teams, national leaders, executive team) to align the entire company in 45 minutes.
- **Who Runs the Meeting:** A naturally structured and disciplined person (not necessarily the CEO) to keep time (use countdown stopwatch) and redirect tangents offline.

#### ðŸ“‹ Quick Daily Agenda (5-15 minutes):

1. **Whatâ€™s up? (2â€“5 minutes):** Each person shares their #1 priority for the next 24 hours, activities, meetings, accomplishments, noteworthy customer news. Reveals conflicts and missed opportunities.
2. **Daily measurements/indicators (2â€“5 minutes):** Each person reports daily metrics from the previous day and current goal. Focus on measurable behavior (e.g., website hits, proposals, accounts receivable).
3. **Where are you stuck? (2â€“5 minutes):** Each person shares their specific barrier/constraint/bottleneck. This identifies sticking points, critical for solving problems. Productive people get stuck regularly; "Everything is fine!" indicates a potential problem.
4. **Optional: Review a core ideology.**

**Coaching question:** "What is your #1 goal and what's in your way of achieving it?" This helps identify coachable moments and barriers to priorities. Remember: **do not problem-solve in daily meetings**; take it offline.

### ðŸ—“ï¸ The Weekly Meeting Agenda (30-60 Minutes)

The weekly meeting is more issues-oriented and strategic. Daily huddles resolve immediate fires, allowing weekly meetings to focus on quarterly goals.

#### âš™ï¸ Weekly Meeting Structure

- **Schedule:** Same time, same place each week. 30 minutes for frontline, 1 hour for execs. Zoom calls for distributed teams.
- **Relevancy:** Essential for focusing on important issues, and once established, employees find it crucial for proper functioning.

#### ðŸ“‹ Suggested Weekly Agenda:

1. **Good News (5 minutes):** Everyone shares one personal and one business good news story from the past week. Counters negativity, builds relationships, and serves as a mental health check.
2. **Customer and Employee Data (10 minutes):** Review recurring issues/concerns from feedback logs. Assign a person or small group to explore one issue for root cause.
3. **The Numbers (10 minutes):** Review individual/team weekly productivity measures. Display graphically to show trends.
4. **A Rock, or Single Issue (10-30 minutes):** Focus collective intelligence on one large priority ("Rock") for the month/quarter. The accountable person presents on their approach. Rotate topics to tackle 15-20 Rocks annually.
5. **One-Phrase Closes:** Each attendee shares a word/phrase about how they feel about the meeting. Formalizes closing, ensures everyone's voice heard, reveals lingering issues.
6. **Keep a Log:** Record who committed to do what, when.

Weekly meetings, if effective, make jobs easier and more productive. They are crucial for maintaining rhythm.

### ðŸ“š The Monthly Meetingâ€”Agenda Is Learning (2-4 Hours)

Focuses on **learning** and passing "DNA" down to middle leadership. This 2-4 hour meeting for the extended middle and senior leadership team involves:

- Reviewing progress on priorities.
- Detailed review of monthly P&L.
- Discussing process effectiveness (what's working/not working).
- Making appropriate adjustments.
- 1-2 hours of specific training.

Involving middle leadership is critical for their growth and alignment.

### â“ "Why So Many Meetings?"

Daily and weekly meetings are superior to one-on-one sessions:

- **Accountability:** Peer pressure drives deliverables better than individual reporting to CEO.
- **Collective Intelligence:** Team's combined brainpower is more effective than one-on-one problem-solving (like "Who Wants to Be a Millionaire?" audience vs. phone-a-friend).
- **Reinforcement:** Opportunity to reinforce core ideologies and recognize efforts.

Routine, rhythm, and consistent daily/weekly meetings provide certainty and prevent companies from drifting off course, even amidst rapid change.

A brand promise is the **single most important measurable** that sets a company apart. It drives all executive decisions. A measurable brand promise is crucial, defining the company, galvanizing the organization, and driving strategic and tactical decisions. It's developed by considering BHAG, sandbox, customer needs, and chokepoint control.

## ðŸ’° Chapter 10: Mastering Cash Flow

This chapter highlights cash as the "oxygen that fuels growth" and introduces the Cash Conversion Cycle (CCC) as a key performance indicator. It explores strategies to improve CCC using the One-Page Cash Acceleration Strategies (CASh) tool, enabling firms to fund growth internally and reduce reliance on external financing. Constantly improving cash flow is a powerful driver for overall business improvement.

### ðŸ”„ Cash Conversion Cycle (CCC)

The **Cash Conversion Cycle (CCC)** measures the time (in days) it takes for a dollar spent by the company to flow back into its bank account. A negative CCC means the company receives cash before it has to spend it.

- **Dell Computer Example:** Michael Dell experienced "growing broke" despite rapid growth. CFO Tom Meredith reduced Dell's CCC from 63 days to a negative 21 days by focusing on one cash improvement strategy every 90 days. This meant Dell generated cash as it grew faster, providing internal funding for growth.
- **Catapult Systems LLC:** Improved cash flow by billing clients twice per month instead of a 30-day cycle, as 90% of clients agreed to the change, nearly doubling cash flow immediately.

To understand and improve CCC, recommended reading includes "How Fast Can Your Company Afford to Grow?" (HBR article by Churchill and Mullins) and "The Customer-Funded Business" (by Mullins).

#### CASh: Cash Acceleration Strategies Tool

The one-page **Cash Acceleration Strategies (CASh)** tool helps brainstorm ways to improve CCC by breaking it into four main components:

![Diagram showing the Cash Conversion Cycle components: Sales Cycle, Make/Production & Inventory Cycle, Delivery Cycle, and Billing & Payment Cycle.](https://i.imgur.com/k2x2qL3.png)Most businesses have aspects of each, though their sequence might differ (e.g., Dell collecting payment before production).

Management teams should dedicate an hour or more monthly to brainstorm improvements for each cash cycle component, ideally with the broader middle-leadership team, to enhance understanding of cash flow and contributions.

#### Opportunities for Cash Flow Improvement:

- **Challenge assumptions:** Stop saying, "This is just the way it is in our industry."
- **Daily cash reporting:** Track available cash daily, with explanations for changes, charted against accounts receivable (AR) and accounts payable (AP) weekly.
- **Ask for faster payment:** Small firms find large companies will often pay faster or prepay if simply asked.
- **Value for on-time payment:** Offer incentives or value-adds for customers who pay on time or in advance.
- **Faster invoicing:** Hire dedicated accounting staff to ensure timely invoicing and follow-up.
- **Friendly reminders:** Send reminders 5 days before payment deadlines.
- **Automate recurring payments:** Obtain recurring credit card authorization for recurring invoices.
- **Understand late payments:** Investigate reasons for late payments (product/service dissatisfaction, invoicing errors, misalignment with customer's invoicing system).
- **Align billing with customer cycles:** Time your billings to coincide with customer payment cycles.
- **Leverage credit cards:** Pay your own expenses with credit cards to extend float; accept credit card payments from customers for faster collection.
- **Help customers improve cash flow:** Offer leasing options to help them pay on time.
- **Shorten product/service delivery cycles:** Faster completion of "work in progress" leads to faster payment.
- **Offer valuable products/services:** Create leverage to ask customers to pay sooner.
- **Improve margins and profit:** Higher profitability directly improves cash flow.

**CFO Cash Report:** CFOs or controllers should provide a daily cash report summarizing inflows/outflows and anticipated cash flow, enabling quick reactions to negative trends and insights into the business's financial model.

#### General Categories for Improvements:

1. **Shorten cycle times.**
2. **Eliminate mistakes.**
3. **Change the business model.**

These categories are further explored for stimulating thinking.

### â³ Shorten Cycle Times

Increasing the pace of all company activities, from customer interest to transaction completion, improves CCC. Applying **Toyota's Lean process** (eliminating wasted time) is ideal for improving processes, employee productivity, and cash flow.

- **Sales process:** Reduce sales cycles. Negotiation techniques (e.g., Victoria Medvec's "High Stakes Negotiation" course) can shorten cycles from months to weeks/days.
- **Production side:** Dell's production workers assembled computers in minutes, with only days of inventory, contributing to impressive CCC.
- **Accounting departments:** Often understaffed, leading to invoicing/collection delays. Catapult Systems' collections specialist built personal rapport with clients' AP teams, calling 5 days before due date, leading to "unbelievably high" on-time payment. Offering small thank-yous (e.g., lottery tickets) to AP clerks can prioritize your invoices.
- **Specific due dates:** Specify exact due dates (e.g., May 31) instead of "due in 30 days" to bypass internal client sign-off delays.
- **Holistic process review:** Examine sales, production, service delivery, billing, and collections for speed improvements.

### âŒ Eliminate Mistakes

Mistakes infuriate customers and cause slow payments. Incomplete orders, invoicing errors, and missed deadlines are costly and snarl cash flow.

- **Salisbury Landscaping Example:** Optimized CCC by securing upfront deposits, finishing jobs quickly (one job at a time), and performing walk-throughs with customers to immediately address deficiencies ("adjustments"). This built a reputation for efficiency, making collections easier.
- **PPR Talent Management Group Example:** Freed up $1M/month by improving invoicing accuracy. Hired an additional person to customize invoices for each hospital client, increasing client confidence.

### âš™ï¸ Change the Business Model

Adjustments to the business model can significantly affect CCC.

- **PPR:** Asked customers to pay in advance for growth capital; many agreed.
- **Customer-funded business:** Get customers to fund your business (e.g., Costco memberships).
- **Supplier-funded business:** Have suppliers fund inventory (e.g., Dell's inventory management).

Recommended reading: Verne Harnish's "Finding Money You Didn't Know You Had" article.

### ðŸ’¸ Improving Profitability

Improving profitability directly improves cash flow, especially when avoiding "management waste" on the balance sheet.

- **Benetton India Example:** Implemented online bidding for vendor contracts (Ariba) during a downturn, saving $1.2M annually (e.g., shopping bags from 52 to 34 cents) while maintaining quality.
- **MOM's Organic Market:** Focused on improving profitability (pricing, purchasing) during financial crisis, driving free operating cash flow to fund expansion.
- **Cost scrutiny:** Regularly scrutinize all goods and services payments. Goodner (Catapult Systems) saved thousands by eliminating recurring charges like bottled water service.

#### Completing Your CASh Tool:

1. Read "How Fast Can Your Company Afford to Grow?" (Churchill & Mullins).
2. Calculate existing CCC in days.
3. Calculate cash required to fund each additional day of CCC.
4. Brainstorm ways to improve CCC and the 7 financial levers (shorten cycle times, eliminate mistakes, change business model) for each segment of CCC.
5. Choose one cash-improvement initiative every 90 days as a quarterly priority (Rock).

Improving CCC by 30 days in a $30M business can free up $2.5M, which can be used to pay down credit, distribute dividends, invest in new projects, or save for tough times. This makes the business pulse faster, improves managers' financial savvy, and allows better sleep when scaling.

### ä¼šè¨ˆ Accounting: Underappreciated

Accounting is often underappreciated, viewed as a necessary evil. However, dedicating more attention and resources to accounting can significantly improve profits and cash (John D. Rockefeller was an accountant).

3. Trend analysis and early-warning systems for better prediction.

#### ðŸ“Š Waterfall Graphs

Accountants should create waterfall graphs to slice and dice financial data (gross margin, profit, cash flow) by categories (customer, location, product line, salesperson, SKU). - **Turnaround specialists (e.g., Greg Brenneman):** Use this data to eliminate unprofitable routes (Continental Airlines) and menu items (Quiznos).
- **Strategic Decisions:** Avoid keeping "loss leaders" for "strategic reasons" unless treated as a marketing expense. Steve Jobs eliminated Apple's money-losing handheld computers.

#### ðŸ“ˆ Trend Analysis

Leadership's responsibility is prediction, requiring frequent quantitative data and qualitative market feedback. Data older than a week is history. Accounting should provide reports and graphs that help predict the near future.

- **Electrical supplies distributor:** Weekly bar charts tracking major customers' purchases (80% of revenue) revealed a customer slowly reducing order size, allowing proactive follow-up. Also identified changes in product line popularity faster.

#### ðŸ“ Mapping Software

#### ðŸ’² Gross Margin Dollars

Focus on **Gross Margin Dollars** (revenue minus all NONLABOR direct costs) as the "true economic top line" instead of vanity revenue. This is especially important for businesses with subcontractors, high material costs, or low margins.

- In most service companies, Gross Margin Dollars becomes the new top-line focus, driving opportunities for the highest gross margin per labor dollar, crucial for moving beyond breakeven to 10%+ profitability.
- **Andy Bailey (NationLink Wireless):** Drove Gross Margin Dollars per employee from $75,000 to $275,000 (4x industry average), leading to an outsized valuation.
- Gross margin tends to shrink as companies scale (due to market demand for better pricing, increased complexities, higher costs). A few percentage points dip at $10M-$100M can mean $300,000-$3M unavailable for infrastructure.

Two options to improve gross margins:

1. **Refine strategy:** Maintain differentiation to hold pricing (requires salespeople selling differentiation, marketing focusing on right customers). This can increase gross margins with growth (hyperspecialization).
2. **Cost-led pricing:** If market dictates price, adjust costs to fit and still profit (drive up overall gross margin dollars, acknowledging fixed costs).

**WARNING:** Never base sales compensation on revenue unless cost of goods sold is fixed, otherwise salespeople may set prices too low. Gross margin is the most powerful indicator of an effective sales team and differentiated strategy.
